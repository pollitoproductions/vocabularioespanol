Spanish articles: el, la, un, una, and lo

Articles are the small words that come before nouns, like “the” and “a” in English. Spanish has more of them than English, because articles match the noun’s gender and number, and Spanish also uses them in places where English would not. This guide covers every type you are likely to meet. For the basics of masculine and feminine nouns, see El and la: Spanish noun gender.

Definite articles: the

  • El (masculine singular): el libro
  • La (feminine singular): la casa
  • Los (masculine plural): los libros
  • Las (feminine plural): las casas

Use them for specific things you and the listener both know, just as in English: Dame el lápiz (give me the pencil).

Indefinite articles: a, an, some

  • Un (masculine singular): un perro
  • Una (feminine singular): una mesa
  • Unos (masculine plural): unos perros (some dogs)
  • Unas (feminine plural): unas mesas (some tables)

These introduce something new or unspecified: Tengo un gato.

The neuter article lo

Lo is used with an adjective to talk about an idea instead of a specific noun: lo bueno (the good part), lo importante (the important thing), lo difícil (what is hard). It has no gender and no plural. It also appears in lo que, meaning “what” or “the thing that”: Entiendo lo que dices.

Al and del

When a or de comes right before el, the two words join: a + el = al and de + el = del. Voy al parque. Es el libro del profesor. This never happens with la, los, or las, and it does not apply when El is part of a name, such as de El Paso.

El before feminine nouns

A feminine singular noun that starts with a stressed a sound takes el or un to make the sounds easier to say: el agua, el águila, un hacha. The noun stays feminine, so adjectives remain feminine: el agua fría. In the plural, you go back to las aguas.

Where Spanish uses an article and English does not

  • General ideas and categories: Me gustan los gatos. La vida es corta.
  • Days of the week: El lunes tengo clase. (On Monday I have class.)
  • Times: Son las tres. Es la una.
  • Body parts and clothing: Me lavo las manos. Se pone el abrigo. Spanish uses the article, not “my” or “his.”
  • A language as the subject: El español es divertido.
  • Titles when talking about someone: La señora López es amable.
  • Prices and rates: Cuesta dos euros el kilo. (Two euros per kilo.)

Where Spanish leaves the article out

  • Languages after hablar, estudiar, or aprender: Hablo español. Estudio francés.
  • Professions and nationalities after ser: Mi madre es doctora. Somos mexicanos. If you add a description, the article returns: Es una doctora excelente.
  • Talking directly to someone with a title: Buenos días, señora López.
  • Many set phrases with prepositions: en casa, en clase, a casa, de vacaciones.
  • Unspecified things after tener, necesitar, or hay in the negative: No tengo coche. Necesito ayuda.

Tips for learning articles

  • Learn each noun with its article, as the game shows them.
  • Notice the pattern: definite articles point to something known, indefinite articles introduce something new.
  • When English says “my” for a body part or piece of clothing, try the article instead.
  • When a rule seems to disagree with English, remember that it is a habit of the language, not a mistake.

Why articles are worth the effort

Articles are tiny, and learners are tempted to skip them, but they carry real information. They tell you the gender and number of the noun, they show whether the speaker means a specific thing or a general one, and they help the listener follow the sentence. A Spanish speaker who hears el problema knows the noun is masculine and singular before the word is even finished. Dropping or mixing up the article is understandable, yet it is one of the quickest ways to sound unnatural, so it is worth building the habit early.

Seeing articles in the game

Every noun card in the game shows the article with the noun, so you are seeing the pattern constantly. Use that to your advantage by reading the whole phrase aloud, such as el árbol, la montaña, or las estrellas, instead of only the noun. Then try changing the article to its indefinite partner, saying un árbol, una montaña, and unas estrellas. Moving back and forth between the definite and indefinite forms is a quick way to practice both sets without needing a separate lesson or worksheet.

Articles and plurals

Plural articles follow the noun: if the noun is plural, the article must be too. Los libros, las casas, unos amigos, and unas ideas all show the same agreement. Adjectives join in as well, so la casa blanca becomes las casas blancas. When you practice, change a whole phrase from singular to plural and back again, checking that the article, the noun, and the adjective all change together. It is repetitive but effective, and it quickly reveals which parts of the pattern you still have to think about.

Common slips to watch for

Beginners most often forget the article with general ideas, or they add one where Spanish leaves it out. Saying Me gustan gatos instead of Me gustan los gatos sounds incomplete, while saying Soy un profesor instead of Soy profesor sounds slightly off. Keep a short note of the slips you make, and review it before you write or speak. Because articles are so short, a few extra seconds of attention make a big difference to how natural your Spanish sounds.
