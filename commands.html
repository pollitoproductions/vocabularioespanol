Tú commands

Tú commands are the words you use when you tell someone to do something, and in Spanish the command changes depending on who you are talking to. If you are talking to a friend, a classmate, your brother or sister, or anyone you call “tú,” you use a tú command. “Habla más despacio.” Speak more slowly. “Come tu cena.” Eat your dinner. “Abre la ventana.” Open the window. These are not suggestions. They are direct instructions, and they are some of the most useful sentences you will ever learn, because telling people what to do, politely, is something you do every day. This guide covers the two kinds of tú commands: the affirmative ones, which tell someone to do something, and the negative ones, which tell someone not to do something. The two kinds are formed in completely different ways, and that is the first thing to understand.

Affirmative commands: the form you already know

The affirmative tú command is easy, because you already know it. It is the third-person singular present tense, the él / ella / usted form. If you can say “él habla” (he speaks), you can say “habla” as a command: “Habla con tu profesor.” Talk to your teacher. “Mira la foto.” Look at the photo. “Escucha la música.” Listen to the music. “Come tu cena.” Eat your dinner. “Escribe tu nombre.” Write your name. The command is just the he/she form, used on its own. That is the whole rule for regular verbs, and it works for -ar, -er, and -ir verbs alike. “Limpia tu cuarto.” Clean your room. “Lee el libro.” Read the book. “Abre la ventana.” Open the window.

There is one catch: eight very common verbs do not follow the rule. They have irregular affirmative commands, and you have to memorize them. The eight are: decir → di (say, tell), hacer → haz (do, make), ir → ve (go), poner → pon (put), salir → sal (leave, go out), ser → sé (be), tener → ten (have), and venir → ven (come). “Di la verdad.” Tell the truth. “Haz la tarea.” Do the homework. “Ve a la escuela.” Go to school. “Pon el libro aquí.” Put the book here. “Sal de la casa.” Leave the house. “Sé bueno.” Be good. “Ten paciencia.” Have patience. “Ven a mi casa.” Come to my house. These eight are the verbs you will use most often, so they are worth learning first. Say them out loud a few times, di, haz, ve, pon, sal, sé, ten, ven, and they start to stick. Every other verb follows the third-person rule, so once you know these eight, you can give a command with almost any verb you know.

Stem-changing verbs keep their stem change in the command, because the command comes from the third-person present. “Cierra la puerta.” Close the door. “Empieza ahora.” Start now. “Piensa en la respuesta.” Think about the answer. “Duerme ocho horas.” Sleep eight hours. “Pide ayuda.” Ask for help. “Sigue las instrucciones.” Follow the instructions. “Sirve la sopa.” Serve the soup. “Juega al fútbol.” Play soccer. If you know the third-person present form, you know the command. That is why the affirmative command feels so natural once you have the present tense down.

Negative commands: no plus the subjunctive

Negative commands are formed completely differently. You do not use the third-person present. You use the present subjunctive, and you put “no” in front of it. The subjunctive for a tú command comes from the yo form of the present tense: drop the -o, and add -es for -ar verbs or -as for -er and -ir verbs. “Hablar” becomes yo hablo, drop the -o, add -es, and you get “hables.” “No hables en clase.” Do not talk in class. “Comer” becomes yo como, drop the -o, add -as, and you get “comas.” “No comas eso.” Do not eat that. “Escribir” becomes yo escribo, drop the -o, add -as, and you get “escribas.” “No escribas eso.” Do not write that.

The same eight irregular verbs have irregular negative commands, and they follow the subjunctive pattern: “no digas” (do not say), “no hagas” (do not do), “no vayas” (do not go), “no pongas” (do not put), “no salgas” (do not leave), “no seas” (do not be), “no tengas” (do not have), “no vengas” (do not come). “No digas mentiras.” Do not tell lies. “No hagas eso.” Do not do that. “No vayas a la fiesta.” Do not go to the party. “No seas malo.” Do not be mean. “No tengas miedo.” Do not be afraid. “No vengas tarde.” Do not come late.

Notice that the negative command always starts with “no.” There is no negative command without it. If you leave out the “no,” you have an affirmative command, and the meaning flips completely. “Habla” means “speak.” “No hables” means “do not speak.” The “no” is not optional. It is the whole difference between telling someone to do something and telling them not to do it. And the verb itself changes too: the affirmative uses the third-person present, while the negative uses the subjunctive. “Habla” against “no hables.” “Come” against “no comas.” “Escribe” against “no escribas.” Two different forms, two opposite meanings.

Spelling changes from the present tense carry into the subjunctive. A verb whose yo form ends in -co, like “buscar” (yo busco), keeps that spelling in the subjunctive: “no busques.” A verb like “sacar” (yo saco) becomes “no saques.” “Tocar” (yo toco) becomes “no toques.” “Apagar” (yo apago) becomes “no apagues.” The spelling change is there so the sound stays the same. You are still saying the same consonant sound, just spelled the way Spanish spells it before an -e. The rule is the same as the subjunctive you will meet later: take the yo form, drop the -o, and add the opposite ending. Once you can do that, you can build any negative command.

Pronouns: attached to the affirmative, before the negative

Pronouns, the little words lo, la, me, te, and nos, attach to the end of an affirmative command, and they go before the verb in a negative command. “Hazlo.” Do it. The “lo” sticks to the end of “haz” and becomes one word. “Dime.” Tell me. “Ponlo aquí.” Put it here. When you attach a pronoun to a command, the stress can shift, so you sometimes need a written accent to keep the stress in the right place. “Dime” needs no accent, but a longer command like “cómpralo” (buy it) does, because the stress falls three syllables from the end once the pronoun is attached.

In a negative command, the pronoun goes before the verb, after the “no.” “No lo hagas.” Do not do it. “No me digas nada.” Do not tell me anything. The pronoun never attaches to a negative command. It always sits in front, between “no” and the verb. This is the rule students forget most often, and it is the one that makes your Spanish sound natural when you get it right. Compare the two: “hazlo” (do it) against “no lo hagas” (do not do it). Same pronoun, same verb, completely different position. Affirmative: attached to the end. Negative: before the verb. If you remember that one contrast, you have the pronoun rule.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is using the present-tense tú form instead of the command. “Hablas con tu profesor” is a statement. You are talking to your teacher. “Habla con tu profesor” is a command. Talk to your teacher. The -s at the end is the difference. If your command ends in -s, it is not a command. It is a statement. Listen for that -s. “Comes tu cena” means you are eating your dinner. “Come tu cena” means eat your dinner. One letter changes a statement into a command.

The second mistake is forgetting the “no” in a negative command. “Hables en clase” is not a sentence. It needs the “no”: “No hables en clase.” Without the “no,” the subjunctive form just hangs there with nothing to do. The third mistake is using the affirmative form for a negative. “No habla en clase” is wrong. The negative needs the subjunctive: “No hables en clase.” The fourth mistake is putting the pronoun in the wrong place. “Lo haz” is wrong. It is “hazlo.” “No hazlo” is wrong. It is “no lo hagas.” If you remember the eight irregulars, the “no” plus subjunctive rule, and the pronoun rule, you have the whole system.
