Spanish Dash and Slope Español in the classroom

Spanish Dash and Slope Español are two games that teach the same Spanish: the first half of Spanish 1 — cognates, everyday vocabulary, present-tense conjugation, possessive pronouns, and ser versus estar. They draw from mostly the same word lists, so a word a student meets in one game will usually turn up in the other. The difference is the shape. Dash is a run through ten levels in order, from the easiest words to the hardest sentences. Slope is an endless run that keeps mixing the same material back in, a little faster every time. One is the path through the material; the other is the review that never ends.

Spanish Dash: ten levels in order

Dash is a runner. A little chicken cube runs across the screen on its own, and the player taps, clicks, or presses Space to jump spikes, gaps, and blocks. Every level is built around 25 questions, asked five at a time at five checkpoints spread through the level. Answer all five at a checkpoint and the run goes on. Pick a wrong answer in levels 3 to 10 and the player goes back to the last checkpoint and tries that stretch again. The first two levels are the exception: cognates carry no penalty, so a wrong pick there costs nothing and the run keeps going.

The ten levels follow the order of a first-year course:

  • Levels 1–2 — Cognates. 484 words that look like English and mean the same thing in Spanish, such as el teléfono and la música. A gentle start, with no penalty for a wrong answer.
  • Levels 3–4 — Practice. 607 everyday words from the 1,000-word list, with the cognates taken out, so students are tested on words they have to know rather than words they can guess.
  • Levels 5–6 — Conjugation. Present-tense forms of 113 verbs with five subjects — yo, tú, él/ella, nosotros, and ellos — 565 combinations in all.
  • Levels 7–8 — Possessive pronouns. 380 phrases built on the same nouns with a different pronoun each time — mi gato, tu gato, su gato, nuestro gato — including a tricky distractor that gets the pronoun right and the number wrong.
  • Levels 9–10 — Ser vs. estar. 96 sentences split between ser and estar, each one written so the sentence itself points to the right verb.

Speaking is optional in Dash, and it is off by default. A checkbox on the home menu turns it on, and then the student also says each answer out loud after picking it. If the game mishears the student, they can try again as many times as they like — speaking never blocks the run. The listen button only appears on the speaking step, after the right answer has been picked, so it can never give the answer away.

Slope Español: the same material, mixed

Slope is a three-dimensional endless runner. A ball rolls down a neon track that keeps getting steeper and faster, up to a top speed of 85, and the player steers to dodge red blocks and gaps — or hops over them. There is no finish line and no last level. The run ends when the ball crashes, and that is where the Spanish comes in.

A crash opens a quiz revive: five Spanish questions, and four out of five right keeps the run going. Each run has 50 revives; once they're used, the next crash ends the run, and failing a quiz ends the run too. The revive questions follow a set order in blocks of ten revives: the first ten are cognates, the next ten are common words, then conjugation, then possessive pronouns, then ser versus estar. A student who plays for a while meets every topic, in order.

Word tiles add a second way to practise. A tile on the track shows an emoji, and rolling through it pauses the run and shows the Spanish word with its English meaning. Say the word out loud and the student banks a free revive. The next crash spends the free pass instead of opening a quiz, so collecting words is what keeps a run alive. Hopping over a tile skips it, so students have to choose: play safe, or go after the words.

A run can be saved from the pause menu and picked up later from the home menu, and the game keeps a top-five list of the longest runs on the device — distances and dates only, no names and no accounts. Speaking is optional here too, and off by default.

How they fit together

Use Dash when you want order and Slope when you want review. A student working through Dash is working through the course in sequence, and the level they are on tells you exactly which topic they are practising. A student playing Slope is meeting all five topics again in a set order that cycles through all five topics, which is what keeps older material from fading. Because both games use mostly the same word lists, progress in one shows up in the other: a verb form learned in Dash level 5 can turn up in a Slope revive, and a possessive phrase from Dash level 7 can come back in Slope's fourth block of revives. Neither game replaces the other. Dash is the way through the material; Slope is the way back through it.

Ways to use them in class

Warm-ups. Five minutes of Dash at the start of class is enough for a level or two, and the level number gives the class an obvious goal: today we finish level 3. The questions are the lesson's vocabulary, so the warm-up is already the review.

Review days. Slope is built for these. The revive cycle means a single run touches every topic, and the free passes reward students for collecting words instead of dodging them. Put the game on the board and let the class call out the answers together — the quiz is the same whether one student is playing or thirty are shouting.

Stations. Set Dash up at one station and Slope at another and rotate the class through. One station is ordered practice, the other is mixed review, and students get both without you having to plan two different activities.

Fast finishers. Slope has no end, and the top-five list on the device gives a finished student somewhere to go that is still Spanish. The list holds distances and dates only, so it works as a simple class leaderboard without names or accounts.

Speaking, on your terms. Both games leave speaking off unless you turn it on, so the same game works for a silent warm-up or for pronunciation practice. When speaking is on, the game only asks the student to say the answer after they have already picked it, and a misheard answer just means trying again.

Practical notes. Neither game needs an account or a sign-up, and both run in the browser — on the classroom board, a Chromebook, or a phone. Slope's save-and-continue means a run can stop at the bell and pick up next lesson. Dash remembers how far a student has got — the highest level unlocked and the best result on each level — so a student can come back and carry on where they left off.

The grammar behind the games

This guide does not re-teach the grammar — the guides for each topic do that, and the games are the practice for what those guides explain. For the words themselves, start with the cognates guide and the full vocabulary list. For verb forms, the conjugation guide and the verb conjugation tool cover the same 113 verbs. Ser versus estar has its own guide, and possessive pronouns are covered in the pronouns guide. If a student is stuck on a level, the answer is usually in one of those guides, not in playing the level again.

Both games are free, with nothing to download and nothing to sign up for. Play Spanish Dash or play Slope Español — or set the class a level of Dash to warm up and a run of Slope to finish.
