Pick ser or estar

Ser vs. estar: two ways to say “to be”

English has one verb for “to be.” Spanish has two: ser and estar. Choosing between them is one of the first big hurdles for learners, but a few simple ideas cover most situations. The short version is this: ser tells you what something is, and estar tells you how or where something is right now.

The present tense forms

  • Ser: yo soy, tú eres, él/ella/usted es, nosotros somos, vosotros sois, ellos/ustedes son
  • Estar: yo estoy, tú estás, él/ella/usted está, nosotros estamos, vosotros estáis, ellos/ustedes están

Both are irregular, so they are worth memorizing early. Notice the accent marks on estás, está, estáis, and están.

Use ser for identity and lasting qualities

A popular memory trick is the word DOCTOR, which stands for Description, Occupation, Characteristic, Time, Origin, and Relationship.

  • Description and characteristics: Mi perro es grande. Ella es simpática.
  • Occupation: Mi hermana es ingeniera. Note that Spanish usually leaves out “a” or “an” here.
  • Time and dates: Hoy es martes. Son las tres.
  • Origin and nationality: Somos de Colombia.
  • Relationships: Carlos es mi primo.
  • Material and ownership: La mesa es de madera. El libro es de Ana.
  • Where an event takes place: La fiesta es en mi casa.

Use estar for location and conditions

The word PLACE works the same way: Position, Location, Action, Condition, and Emotion.

  • Location of people and things: Madrid está en España. El libro está en la mesa.
  • Position: Estamos sentados.
  • Actions in progress: Estoy estudiando. This is estar plus a verb ending in -ando or -iendo.
  • Conditions: La sopa está caliente. Las ventanas están abiertas.
  • Emotions and states: Estoy cansado. Ellos están contentos.

Notice the difference between where a thing is and where an event happens. A building or a city uses estar, even though it never moves: El museo está en el centro. A party or a concert uses ser: El concierto es en el parque.

Same adjective, different meaning

Some adjectives change meaning depending on the verb, which shows how the two verbs think differently.

  • Ser aburrido means to be boring, while estar aburrido means to be bored.
  • Ser listo means to be clever, while estar listo means to be ready.
  • Ser malo means to be a bad person or thing, while estar malo means to be sick.
  • Ser rico means to be wealthy, while estar rico means to taste delicious.
  • Ser seguro means to be safe, while estar seguro means to be sure.
  • Ser verde means to be green, while estar verde means to be unripe.

How to decide

Ask yourself one question: am I describing what this is like in general, or how it is at this moment? Es nervioso says he is a nervous person. Está nervioso says he is nervous right now, perhaps because of a test. Eres alto is a fact about you, while Estás muy alto is what you might say to a child who has grown a lot since you last saw them.

When you are unsure, also check for the quick signals: a place usually means estar, a profession, nationality, or time usually means ser, and a verb ending in -ando or -iendo always means estar.

Common mistakes

  • Wrong: Estoy de Chile. Right: Soy de Chile. Origin always uses ser.
  • Wrong: Soy cansado. Right: Estoy cansado. Tiredness is a condition, not a personality trait.
  • Wrong: Soy en la biblioteca. Right: Estoy en la biblioteca. Location of a person uses estar.

A practice idea

When you collect an adjective card in the game, write two short sentences with it, one using ser and one using estar, and decide which one is natural. With abierto, for example, a store that is open right now is La tienda está abierta, because being open is a condition. Working out why one version sounds right and the other does not builds your instinct faster than memorizing rules alone.

Why Spanish has two verbs

It can seem unnecessary to have two verbs where English has one, but the split lets Spanish say something English has to explain with extra words. Es aburrido tells you what someone or something is like, while está aburrido tells you how they feel at the moment. The two verbs give speakers a quick way to separate lasting qualities from temporary conditions, and once you get used to the idea, many learners find that the distinction is useful instead of annoying.

Practice with questions

Questions are a good way to practice, because the verb you use shapes the answer. ¿Cómo eres? asks about your personality or appearance, while ¿Cómo estás? asks how you feel right now. ¿De dónde eres? asks about origin, and ¿Dónde estás? asks about location. Answer each question aloud in a full sentence, and notice how the verb and the question word work together. If you slip, you can usually catch yourself by asking whether the question is about what something is like or about where and how it is at the moment.
