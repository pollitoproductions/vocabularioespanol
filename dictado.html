Dictation (Dictado)

Hear it, then pick it. Press Hear, listen, and choose what you heard from four similar options. Three tabs: the 1,000 vocabulary words, the 113 verb forms, and the 135 expressions. Below the tool, a short guide to why hearing-first practice works.

The dictation tool

Listening practice only: dictation never marks anything as known. Your Known words tab is only affected by the practice quiz, so nothing here changes it. The vocab tab draws from the same 1,000 words as the games, but answering here is practice, not a known-word test.

Why listening comes first

Children learn their first language by listening for a long time before they say much, and the same order works for a second language. Reading Spanish is kind to learners because words sound mostly the way they look, but listening is where Spanish gets fast. Words blur together, endings get quiet, and two words that look different on paper can sound almost the same in speech. Dictation trains exactly that skill: your ear, not your eye, has to do the work.

How dictation works here

Press the big Hear button and listen. Nothing else shows until you have heard the item at least once, because guessing from spelling is not listening. Then pick what you heard from four similar choices, and use Hear again as often as you like. There is no typing and no speaking in this tool, on purpose. Listening is its own skill, and it deserves practice that is only listening. The three tabs cover the three pools you already study: the 1,000 vocabulary words, the 113 verb forms, and the 135 expressions.

Listen for the endings

In Spanish, endings carry a lot of meaning. A single vowel can change who is doing something, and a verb ending can change when it happened. That is why the choices here are similar on purpose. In the verbs tab, the four options are forms of the same verb, so only the ending tells them apart. Hearing the difference between hablo and habla, or between hablé and habló, is a small skill with a big payoff: once your ear catches endings, whole sentences start to make sense faster. The same goes for word stress and for sounds like ñ and the rolled r, which the Accents and pronunciation guide explains.

What to listen for

Three things are worth your attention. First, word endings, as above. Second, word stress: Spanish words stress one syllable, and the stress can change the word, so listen for which syllable sounds strongest. Third, word boundaries: in fast speech, the end of one word runs into the start of the next, so a phrase you know can sound like one long word. Hearing it again slowly, then at normal speed, helps your ear learn where one word ends and the next begins. None of this needs to be perfect. Dictation is not a test of talent. It is training, and every round makes the next one a little easier.

A routine that works

Five minutes is plenty. Pick one tab, listen to each item twice before choosing, and after you answer, press Hear again and listen to the correct form once more. That second listen is where the learning locks in, because now you know what you are listening for. When a word keeps tripping you up, go meet it in the tool where you first learned it: the practice tool for words, the conjugation tool for verb forms, the idioms tool for expressions. Then come back and see if your ear catches it next time. Listening gets sharper the same way everything else does, a little at a time, and every Spanish moment in your day helps, as Immersion without moving explains.
