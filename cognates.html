    Cognates: Spanish words you already know

    A cognate is a word in two languages that looks similar and means the same thing, because the two languages share a common source. English and Spanish have thousands of them, which means you already know a surprising amount of Spanish before you start. Words like hotel, animal, familia, and chocolate work almost the same in both languages.

    Why English and Spanish share so many words

    Spanish comes from Latin, and a large share of English vocabulary also comes from Latin, mostly through French. That shared history created many pairs of words. The longer and more formal an English word is, the more likely it has a Spanish cousin: opportunity is oportunidad, community is comunidad, and experience is experiencia.

    Patterns that help you spot them

    Many cognates follow predictable endings. Learn these and you can guess hundreds of words:

    • -tion becomes -ción: information → información, station → estación, education → educación
    • -ty becomes -dad: university → universidad, community → comunidad, possibility → posibilidad
    • -ous becomes -oso: famous → famoso, nervous → nervioso, delicious → delicioso
    • -ic becomes -ico: fantastic → fantástico, romantic → romántico, public → público
    • -ly becomes -mente: finally → finalmente, probably → probablemente, directly → directamente
    • -ant and -ent become -ante and -ente: important → importante, different → diferente, elegant → elegante
    • -ary becomes -ario: vocabulary → vocabulario, necessary → necesario, secretary → secretario
    • -ist becomes -ista: artist → artista, pianist → pianista, guitarist → guitarrista
    • -ure becomes -ura: culture → cultura, literature → literatura, structure → estructura
    • -ize becomes -izar: organize → organizar, utilize → utilizar

    Spelling changes to expect

    Spanish usually respells cognates in small ways. Ph becomes f (photograph → fotografía), th becomes t (theater → teatro), and double letters often become single (different → diferente). English words that start with s followed by a consonant often gain an e in front: special → especial, student → estudiante, study → estudiar. Many cognates also add an accent mark, such as música and teléfono.

    Same spelling, different sound

    A cognate may look familiar but still sound different, and the stress often lands in a new place. Hotel is stressed on the last syllable (ho-TEL), hospital is os-pi-TAL, and chocolate is cho-co-LA-te. Say each one with Spanish vowels, not English ones, and check the pronunciation guide if you are unsure.

    Watch out for false friends

    Not every look-alike is a true cognate. Embarazada means pregnant, éxito means success, and librería means bookstore. These are called false friends, and they are worth learning on purpose. See the guide to common mistakes for a longer list.

    How to use cognates when you study

    • When you meet a new Spanish word, guess its English meaning before you check.
    • Keep a separate page in your notebook for cognates and add the pattern next to each word.
    • Try a “cognate hunt”: play for ten minutes and write down every card whose Spanish word looks familiar.
    • Use cognates to build sentences early, since you already know what they mean.

    How to use cognates when you learn

    Cognates give you a head start, but only if you use them on purpose. When you meet a new Spanish word, say it out loud and ask whether it sounds like an English word you already know. If it does, check the meaning, because you may have guessed correctly, and then note the pattern. Over time you will build a mental conversion table, so that seeing a Spanish word ending in -ción immediately suggests an English word ending in -tion. This turns every new cognate into a tool for guessing dozens more.

    Reading is easier than speaking

    Cognates are especially helpful when you read. A short news article or a product label may contain many words you recognize at once, which lets you follow the general meaning even when the grammar is unfamiliar. Speaking is trickier, because you have to produce the Spanish spelling and the Spanish sound, not just recognize a look-alike. That is why it is worth saying cognates aloud: the English pronunciation does not carry over, and practicing the Spanish version builds the habit you need in conversation.

    A cautious approach to guessing

    Guessing from English is a good strategy, but treat it as a hypothesis, not a fact. If a sentence does not make sense with your guess, stop and look the word up. Learners who guess too confidently end up with false friends in their vocabulary, which can be embarrassing. Keep a short list of the surprises you find, because the words that fooled you once are the ones you are most likely to remember correctly afterward.
