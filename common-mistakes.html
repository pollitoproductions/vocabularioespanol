Common mistakes English speakers make in Spanish

Everyone makes mistakes when learning a language, and most of them are predictable. Knowing the common ones ahead of time makes them easier to catch. Here are the ones that trip up English speakers most often.

False friends

Some Spanish words look like English words but mean something different:

  • embarazada means pregnant, not embarrassed (that is avergonzado).
  • éxito means success, not exit (that is salida).
  • librería is a bookstore, while a library is a biblioteca.
  • largo means long, not large (that is grande).
  • actualmente means currently, not actually.
  • asistir means to attend, not to assist.

Forgetting gender

Using the wrong article, like la problema, is one of the most common errors. Learn every noun with el or la, as the game shows it.

Translating word for word

Spanish often says things differently. To give your age, Spanish says Tengo diez años (I have ten years), not “I am ten.” To say you are cold or hungry, use tener: Tengo frío, Tengo hambre. To say you like something, use gustar: Me gusta el café, and for more than one thing, Me gustan los libros.

Mixing up ser and estar

Both mean “to be.” Use ser for identity and lasting traits (Soy profesor) and estar for location and conditions that can change (Estoy cansado, Estoy en casa).

Dropping accents and punctuation

Accent marks are part of the spelling, so si and sí are different words. Spanish questions and exclamations also start with an upside-down mark: ¿Cómo estás? ¡Qué bien!

Capitalization

Unlike English, Spanish does not capitalize days of the week, months, languages, or nationalities: lunes, marzo, español, mexicano.

Adjective placement

Adjectives usually come after the noun and match its gender and number: la casa blanca, los libros nuevos.

More false friends

  • recordar means to remember, not to record
  • pretender means to try or to intend, not to pretend
  • soportar means to put up with, not to support
  • molestar means to bother, not to molest
  • realizar means to carry out, not to realize
  • colegio is a school, not a college
  • lectura is reading, not a lecture
  • sensible means sensitive, not sensible
  • constipado means having a cold
  • carpeta is a folder, not a carpet

Prepositions hidden inside verbs

Some English verbs need a small word that Spanish already includes. Esperar means to wait for, buscar means to look for, escuchar means to listen to, and mirar means to look at. So you say Escucho la música, with no extra word.

Leaving out the subject

Spanish speakers usually skip words like yo and tú, because the verb ending already shows who is acting. Hablo español is more natural than Yo hablo español. Add the pronoun only for emphasis or contrast.

Double negatives

In English, two negatives cancel out. In Spanish they work together: No tengo nada (I don’t have anything) and No veo a nadie (I don’t see anyone) are correct and normal.

How to catch your own mistakes

  • Keep a short list of errors you make and review it weekly.
  • Ask a teacher or a fluent speaker to check a few sentences.
  • Don’t be afraid of errors. Using the language, even imperfectly, is how you improve.

Why mistakes are useful

Mistakes are not a sign that you are failing. They show you where your picture of the language does not yet match the real thing, and each correction fixes a small piece of that picture. Learners who are willing to speak and write imperfectly usually improve faster than those who wait until they feel ready. The goal is not to avoid every error but to notice them sooner, and the list above gives you a head start on the most common ones.

Why English speakers make these errors

Most of these errors come from carrying English habits into Spanish. English has no grammatical gender for objects, so remembering el and la feels unnatural. English uses one verb for “to be” and one for “to know,” so the splits between ser and estar and between saber and conocer feel arbitrary. And English word order and idioms pull learners toward word-for-word translation. Understanding that the mistakes follow a pattern makes them easier to fix, because you can look for the habit behind the error, not just memorize the correction.

A simple correction routine

Write a few sentences each day, then reread them the next morning with fresh eyes, checking them against the list above. Ask yourself four quick questions: did every noun get the right article, did I choose the right “to be,” did I translate anything word for word, and did I include the accents? If you can, ask a teacher or a Spanish speaker to look at a sample once a week. Over a month you will see the same few mistakes shrinking, which is a clear and motivating sign of progress.
