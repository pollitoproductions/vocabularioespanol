What conjugation is

A verb in its dictionary form is called the infinitive: hablar, comer, vivir. Conjugation is what happens when you fit that verb to a person and a time. Hablar becomes hablo when I speak, hablas when you speak, habla when she speaks. English does a little of this — I speak, he speaks — but Spanish does it for every person, every time. That is why a single Spanish verb can wear a dozen different endings, and why learning the patterns matters more than memorizing one form at a time.

Why present and preterite first

Present tense is the tense you live in: what you do, what you like, what is happening right now. Preterite is the tense for finished past actions: what you did yesterday, what happened last year, how the story ended. Together they cover most of everyday storytelling. Other tenses build on these two — the imperfect describes ongoing past scenes, the future talks about what comes next — and the guide to conjugation and the infinitive gives a first look at those. Start here, and the rest gets easier.

The pattern underneath

Most verbs are regular, which is good news: learn the endings for -ar, -er, and -ir verbs and you can conjugate hundreds of verbs you have never studied. Stem-changing verbs keep the same endings but change a vowel in the stem — pensar becomes pienso, dormir becomes duermo — except in the nosotros form, where the stem stays plain: pensamos, dormimos. The preterite has its own endings, plus a few spelling rules that keep pronunciation steady: buscar becomes busqué and llegar becomes llegué, not because the verbs are irregular, but because Spanish spelling changes to protect the sound. The Spanish verbs guide walks through the families and the regular present tense.

Irregulars are the most common verbs

The verbs you use most are the least regular, and that is not bad luck. Words people say every day wear down into irregular shapes in every language. Ser, estar, ir, tener, and hacer all have to be learned as whole tables, not derived from rules. Two pairs deserve their own guides because English uses one word where Spanish uses two: ser vs. estar for “to be” and saber vs. conocer for “to know.” If you only memorize five verbs this month, make it those.

How to use this tool

Pick a tense — present, preterite, or mixed — and a focus: all verbs, irregulars only, or stem-changing only. Each question gives you a verb, a subject, and four forms of that same verb. Pick the right one, then say it: the Say it button listens and gives feedback, but it never blocks you from moving on. Hearing the form matters as much as picking it, so use the Hear it button on every round. Five minutes a day beats an hour once a week, and the more Spanish moments you build into your day, the faster these forms start to feel automatic — which is why this page pairs well with Immersion without moving.
