What conjugation is

A verb in its dictionary form is called the infinitive: hablar, comer, vivir. Conjugation is what happens when you fit that verb to a person and a time. Hablar becomes hablo when I speak, hablas when you speak, habla when she speaks. English does a little of this — I speak, he speaks — but Spanish does it for every person, every time. That is why a single Spanish verb can wear a dozen different endings, and why learning the patterns matters more than memorizing one form at a time.

Why present, preterite, and future first

Present tense is the tense you live in: what you do, what you like, what is happening right now. Preterite is the tense for finished past actions: what you did yesterday, what happened last year, how the story ended. Future is the tense for what comes next: plans, promises, predictions. Together the three cover most of everyday storytelling. Other tenses build on these — the imperfect describes ongoing past scenes, the conditional talks about what would happen — and the guide to conjugation and the infinitive gives a first look at those. Start here, and the rest gets easier.

Regular endings

Regular verbs follow endings, not memorization. In the present, -ar verbs take -o, -as, -a, -amos, -áis, -an (hablo, hablas, habla, hablamos, habláis, hablan); -er verbs take -o, -es, -e, -emos, -éis, -en (como, comes, come, comemos, coméis, comen); -ir verbs take -o, -es, -e, -imos, -ís, -en (vivo, vives, vive, vivimos, vivís, viven). In the preterite, -ar verbs take -é, -aste, -ó, -amos, -asteis, -aron (hablé, hablaste, habló, hablamos, hablasteis, hablaron); -er and -ir verbs take -í, -iste, -ió, -imos, -isteis, -ieron (comí, comiste, comió, comimos, comisteis, comieron). In the future, every verb takes the same endings on the infinitive: -é, -ás, -á, -emos, -éis, -án (hablaré, hablarás, hablará, hablaremos, hablaréis, hablarán) — which makes the future the easiest tense to learn.

Stem changes

Stem-changing verbs keep those endings but change a vowel in the stem. In the present, e becomes ie (pensar → pienso, querer → quiero), o becomes ue (dormir → duermo, poder → puedo), and u becomes ue (jugar → juego) — in every form except nosotros and vosotros, where the stem stays plain (pensamos, pensáis, dormimos, dormís). -Ir stem verbs change again in the preterite, but only in the third person: pedir → pidió, pidieron; dormir → durmió, durmieron; preferir → prefirió; mentir → mintió. Every other preterite form keeps the plain stem.

Spelling changes

A few spelling changes exist only to protect pronunciation. In the yo preterite, -car verbs change c to qu (buscar → busqué), -gar verbs add u (llegar → llegué, jugar → jugué), and -zar verbs change z to c (almorzar → almorcé, abrazar → abracé). In the third-person preterite, creer, leer, and caer change i to y (creyó, creyeron; leyó, leyeron; cayó, cayeron). These are not irregular verbs — the sound stays the same, only the spelling moves.

The future and the irregulars

The future is kinder than the other tenses. Most irregular verbs take regular future endings on their infinitive: seré, iré, estaré, daré, veré, andaré, traeré, conoceré, oiré, caeré. Stem-changing verbs do not change their stems in the future at all: pensaré, dormiré, pediré. Only nine verbs change their stem in the future: tener → tendr- (tendré), poder → podr- (podré), querer → querr- (querré), saber → sabr- (sabré), poner → pondr- (pondré), salir → saldr- (saldré), venir → vendr- (vendré), decir → dir- (diré), and hacer → har- (haré). Learn those nine stems and the future is done. One last shared form to know: ser and ir have the same preterite (fui, fuiste, fue, fuimos, fueron, fuisteis) — context tells you whether it means “was” or “went.” The Spanish verbs guide walks through the families and the regular present tense.

Irregulars are the most common verbs

The verbs you use most are the least regular, and that is not bad luck. Words people say every day wear down into irregular shapes in every language. Ser, estar, ir, tener, and hacer all have to be learned as whole tables, not derived from rules. Two pairs deserve their own guides because English uses one word where Spanish uses two: ser vs. estar for “to be” and saber vs. conocer for “to know.” If you only memorize five verbs this month, make it those. The good news is in the future section above: most of these take regular future endings, so the future tense is mostly already learned.

How to use this tool

Pick a tense — present, preterite, future, or mixed — and a focus: all verbs, irregulars only, or stem-changing only. Each question gives you an English phrase, like “I spoke” or “they will eat,” and four Spanish forms of one verb. Pick the form that matches the person and the tense. The Spanish infinitive stays hidden until you answer, so you have to recognize the form itself, not just match the verb. After you answer, the card shows the infinitive, the English phrase, and the correct form, plus a note when a spelling or stem rule changes the form. Use the Hear it button on every round, and the Say it button to check your own pronunciation — it listens and gives feedback, but it never blocks you from moving on. If you only want the forms used in Latin America, leave “Exclude vosotros” checked; uncheck it to add the vosotros forms used in Spain. Five minutes a day beats an hour once a week, and the more Spanish moments you build into your day, the faster these forms start to feel automatic — which is why this page pairs well with Immersion without moving.
